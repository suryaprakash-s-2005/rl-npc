Abstract—Adaptive strategy-conditioned experience replay—
tagging transitions with the detected play style and sampling from
the matching partition—has been reported to lift the win rate
of reinforcement learning (RL) non-player characters (NPCs) by
+0.25 in a 2-D combat arena. We audit that claim. Surviving
checkpoints reproduce the direction but not the magnitude (∆=
+0.18), and the single-buffer baseline is not reproducible. Rebuilt
with explicit seeding, per-episode records, and a pre-registered
protocol, we calibrate away saturation and run a 36-cell grid
at six paired seeds; the effect is absent (offline −0.069, p=0.25;
online +0.056, p=0.625; median paired difference 0.000). We
report four measured defects: replay capacity is not the controlled
variable its ablation assumes; the oracle-label condition contains
a training-collapse mode with a critic value-scale signature; the
switch point lies beyond the reachable horizon (0/24 episodes);
and the deployed classifier is calibrated to the wrong opponent
(0.836 →0.306), whose retraining raises window-level accuracy
(0.267 →0.802) but not the effect. We release the testbed, pre-
registered audit protocol, and reporting checklist— reproducible
evaluation as sustainable computing.
Index Terms—reinforcement learning, non-player character,
reproducibility, experience replay, evaluation protocol, sustain-
able computing, negative results
I. INTRODUCTION
Non-player characters (NPCs) make a game world feel
inhabited, yet most are driven by hand-written rule tables or
finite-state machines: deterministic controllers a player soon
learns to predict and exploit [1]. Reinforcement learning (RL)
replaces those rules with behaviour learned from interaction
[2]–[4].
We call this the strategy-transition problem: infer which
style the player uses now from delayed, noisy telemetry, detect
when it changes, and adjust play quickly.
Experience replay [4], [5] is the standard reuse mechanism
in deep RL, but a uniform buffer mixes experience from
every strategy and time period, which motivates the strategy-
conditioned partitioned replay we audit: tag every transition
with the active strategy, store it in that strategy’s partition,
and sample preferentially from the partition matching the
currently detected strategy, damped by a parameter-similarity
prior. Earlier work by the present authors (no citable venue)
reported the scheme lifts adaptive win rate from 0.708 to
0.958 in the most demanding condition (labels withheld), with
unchanged adaptation time; we audit that claim here. The
audited system is re-specified in Section III rather than cited.
This paper audits that result and reports that it does
not survive. The defects that make such results fragile—
an unreproducible baseline, a saturated task, an evaluation
horizon the episodes never reach, a classifier calibrated to the
wrong opponent—recur across game-AI RL evaluations and
are detectable from published artifacts alone; an undetected
defect also wastes the re-training it triggers, making repro-
ducible evaluation a sustainability practice [18], [25]–[27].
Contribution 1: a specified system. We design and release
a testbed for mid-encounter strategy adaptation: a symmetric
combat environment with domain-randomized personalities, a
temporal strategy classifier, a confirm-gated change detector,
and a replay memory partitioning transitions by the active
strategy with a parameter-similarity sampling prior; its re-
ported benefit is not established.
Contribution 2: an audit of what the system can support.
The originally claimed +0.25 is not recovered from the surviv-
ing checkpoints (∆= +0.18), and the single-buffer baseline is
not reproducible from a fixed checkpoint: its sampling indices
shared an environment RNG stream, so five repeats of one
checkpoint span a win-rate range of 0.75 while the parti-
tioned arm is exactly reproducible. Under the rebuilt protocol
(explicit seeding, per-episode records) the 36-cell grid at six
paired seeds shows no effect at a calibrated difficulty: offline
−0.069 (p=0.25), online +0.056 (p=0.625), median paired
difference exactly 0.000. Four further transferable defects,
each measured rather than asserted, follow in Section V, with
a reporting checklist to close.

III. THE STRATEGY-CONDITIONED REPLAY SYSTEM
This section specifies our system.
A. Environment and Combat Model
We use a symmetric combat arena, 20 × 20 units, in which
the NPC wins by reducing the player’s health to zero while
keeping its own above zero (∆t = 0.1 s, episodes capped at
400 steps). The action space is |A| = 18 (nine movement
intents × {move, attack}) and the base observation is 14-
dimensional (relative pose, closing rate, healths, cooldowns,
speeds, swing-in-range). cond-on appends a 4-dimensional
one-hot of the active strategy label; cond-off withholds it, so
the agent must infer the strategy from dynamics. Combat is
melee: a hit within attack_range=1.6 inside a 70◦half-
angle cone deals 2 damage, one hit-stun step, on a 4-step
cooldown (contact below 0.5 units ignores the cone). Each
actor has 20 HP as shipped; Section IV raises only the player’s
to 60—the rationale that 20 HP makes a mid-episode switch
observable is contradicted by measurement (Finding F6).
B. Player Strategy Model
A strategy is an 8-tuple (aggression, retreat rate, eva-
sion distance, speed, engagement distance, attack frequency,
attack range, low-health retreat threshold). Four canonical
personalities—aggressive, defensive, evasive, and balanced—
are defined, and each episode’s parameters are perturbed by
multiplicative domain randomization (±20%).
C. Strategy Transition Detection
Temporal strategy classifier. Each step contributes 10
telemetry features over a sliding window of W=15 steps,
fed to a temporal MLP (two 64-unit ReLU layers; 8-window
context; 4-class softmax) trained on 29,920 windows (7,480
per strategy) synthesized against a random-walk opponent.
Confirm-gated CUSUM detector. A two-stage CUSUM
runs on a binary change indicator It = [ˆyt̸ = ˆyseg] with
statistic St = max(0, St−1 + It −k), where k=0.5, alarm
threshold h=6.0, and minimum segment length 10 steps; when
St ≥h a candidate change is armed and confirmed if the
new label persists confirm=10 steps. Classifier scores are
exponentially smoothed (α=0.2) before the argmax, and the
statistic decays to zero when the label is stable.
Implementation note. In the audited code the confirmation
stage gates nothing: the confirmed label filters no training
labels, chooses no partition, and triggers no reprioritization—
transitions are routed by the smoothed argmax label at inser-
tion time.
D. Strategy-Aware Partitioned Replay Memory
Each transition (o, a, r, o′, d) is inserted into the partition
indexed by the active strategy label. At sampling time, with
batch size B and detected strategy c, partition j receives
weight wj(c)=1 if j=c, δ s p
cj if j̸ = c and |Pj| > 0, and 0 if
|Pj| = 0, where scj is the cosine similarity of the canonical
strategy parameters, δ=0.5 the stale-decay floor, and p=1.6
the prior power; the buffer normalizes the weights, draws
(n1, . . . , n4)
∼
Multinomial(B, p1, . . . , p4), and samples
min(nj, |Pj|) from each partition, topping up from the current
one.
E. The RL Agent
The NPC is a Double DQN [17] (two 128-unit hidden lay-
ers; Adam η=5×10−4, gradient-norm clipped at 10; γ=0.99;
soft target τ=0.005 with a 500-step hard sync; batches of 256
every 2 steps; ϵ=0.4 →0.02 over 15,000 steps), pre-filled
with 30 expert demonstrations per strategy. Rewards: +150
(kill), −50 (death), ±8 per hit, +0.6 heading, +1.2 per unit
closure, +1.0 in range, −0.005 per step.
Training runs 400 episodes under a within-episode-switch
protocol: each episode starts under one personality and
switches once to its cyclic partner (aggressive↔defensive,
evasive↔balanced), so the policy must track a transi-
tion.

IV. AUDIT PROTOCOL
A. What We Could Reuse, and What We Could Not
The audit began from the original repository. The per-
episode evaluation records had been discarded, and the legacy
run naming scheme encoded neither the conditioning flag

===== PAGE 3 =====
nor the episode count, so distinct configurations overwrote
one another’s checkpoints: the original ground-truth/cond-
on and all detector-labelled checkpoints are unrecoverable.
With no source-control history we cannot say which revision
produced any surviving number, so the checkpoints are treated
as candidates, re-evaluated under an explicit protocol rather
than assumed provenance.
B. Reproduction Infrastructure
Four infrastructure changes were required before any new
number could be trusted.
Explicit seeding. Python, NumPy, and PyTorch RNGs are
seeded per run; the single-buffer implementation had drawn
sampling indices from the global environment stream and
now has a dedicated one; determinism is verified by exact
reproduction of training curves and statistics. Collision-free
run naming. Every run’s tag encodes replay variant, label
source, conditioning flag, episode count, seed, and a UTC
timestamp. Per-episode records. Every evaluation episode is
written to disk with its win/loss outcome, length, adaptation
status, and detected-transition count; aggregate-only reporting
is what allowed the original result to be misread. A corrected
adaptation metric. The original metric is silent about episodes
ending before the switch—at the original setting the majority
of them (F6). We set the switch to step 40, which 862 of 864
evaluation episodes reach, and report four mutually exclusive
statuses—ok, censored, no-post, short-base— averaging only
ok and censored and reporting the excluded counts, following
the preference for standard reproductions over novel variants.
C. Pre-Registration and the Deciding Run
The protocol for the deciding run was written to a file before
launch and is amended only in the open. The design is a 36-cell
grid: two conditions (ground-truth labels, conditioned on/off)
× three replay arms (single buffer at 200,000, partitioned, and
a single buffer at 50,000) × six paired seeds, at 400 training
and 12 evaluation episodes per cell. The two single-buffer arms
test whether matched total capacity is what matters: a pre-
registration committed to reporting a null or negative outcome
regardless of direction.
D. Task Calibration
The first full grid at the original difficulty showed the
evaluation task was saturated: the frozen policy won every
offline evaluation fight in both arms. A second confound
is excluded—the opponent was caught in 0.89 of training
episodes across the twelve original ground-truth runs, so this
is task balance rather than training budget: the opponent is a
trained controller and the NPC player is a scripted heuristic.
Player HP is the correct knob: symmetric combat damage
changes fight length without shifting win balance, and HP
affects only the player. We swept it and selected 60, the lowest
swept value that moved the offline win rate off the ceiling and
gave the adaptation metric non-degenerate range (Fig. 2).

V. FINDINGS
A. F1: The Baseline Was Not Reproducible
We re-evaluated the surviving single-buffer checkpoints
under a fixed protocol (12 episodes per seed, online evaluation,
6 seeds, the original 20 HP and switch_step=40). The
originally claimed numbers are 0.708 (single) and 0.958
(partitioned), a difference of +0.25. Re-evaluation gives 0.764
and 0.944, a difference of +0.18. The direction survives,
the magnitude does not. These per-seed records are the
−seed<N> files in our artifact tree; the originally reported
switch_step=100 protocol is not among them, so the
comparison is against the originally claimed numbers, not a
like-for-like replay.
The two arms do not share a noise floor: five repeats of the
same evaluation of the same checkpoint (Table I) leave the
partitioned arm bit-for-bit reproducible while the single-buffer
arm’s win rate moves by as much as 0.75. Those runs are
not among our artifacts; their rates are retained as the printed
record.
The cause is an implementation asymmetry, not a property
of the methods: single-buffer sampling drew indices from
a global RNG stream shared with the environment, while
partitioned replay’s per-partition storage incidentally isolated
its sampling. The baseline is thus the noisy arm: the claimed
+0.25 is one draw from a distribution whose per-seed standard
deviation reaches 0.29, compared against a zero-variance arm.

D. F4: Replay Capacity Is Not the Controlled Variable
Partitioned replay is often credited for its larger effective
capacity, so the grid includes a single buffer with one quarter
of the capacity. At the calibrated difficulty this arm moves
the metrics by about as much as the treatment: reducing
single-buffer capacity from 200,000 to 50,000 changes of-
fline win rate by −0.056 (p=0.50) and online win rate by
+0.125 (p=0.25), against partitioning differences of −0.069
and +0.056. At the saturated difficulty the two single-buffer
arms were identical, which is what made capacity look inert.
Neither manipulation then produces a reliable difference, and
the two are of the same magnitude, so a capacity ablation
controls for it only if that effect is measured.
E. F5: The Oracle-Label Condition Contains a Training-
Collapse Mode
The one row of Table II whose interval excludes zero is
the cond-on offline row, an artifact: its exact signed-rank test
(p=0.188) does not exclude zero. Fig. 4 plots training catch
rate in 50-episode blocks: on cond-on seed 5, all three replay
arms fail to learn the task. Catch rate is at most 0.02 in the first
block, the partitioned arm never exceeds 0.06 in any block,
and the two single-buffer arms recover only partially, ending
at 0.50 and 0.20 against 0.84–0.96 for healthy runs.
This is neither a late-training collapse nor a replay effect:
cond-off seed 5 is healthy (0.86–0.92 final catch rate) and
cond-on seed 0 is healthy (0.84–0.96), so the −0.111 cond-on
difference is driven by a training failure, not by replay.
We re-trained all three arms of the failing cell from scratch;
they reproduced the archived training curve exactly, block for

block. Six control cells (cond-off seed 5, cond-on seed 0, all
three arms) also reproduced exactly, so for these nine cells
the collapse is a property of the configuration-seed pair, not
run-to-run variation.
The failure has a clear critic signature on a log scale:
instrumenting the critic on the same nine cells (loss, mean
and maximum absolute online Q-value, TD-target magnitude,
gradient norm at every update; no random numbers drawn;
all 9 cells match block for block against the archived curves)
describes the collapse rather than causing it. In the three failing
arms the mean |Q| is already 4,650–10,025 in the first block
and grows to 7.7 × 104–8.9 × 105 by the last, loss peaking
near 1.3×105; in the six healthy cells the mean |Q| stays 53–
59 in the first block and never exceeds 458, and no healthy
episode’s |Q| exceeds 580 at any point. On the failing arms the
mean |Q| passes 1,000 by episode 22–28, before any catch: a
value-scale divergence of the critic rather than a policy-only
failure, matching the pre-registered suspect—the “Cond-on”
one-hot, which changes instantaneously at a switch, unlike the
other 14 features. We tested two interventions. Regularizing
the critic (Adam weight decay 10−4) does not prevent the
divergence: mean |Q| still reaches 8.1 × 104–2.2 × 105. Re-
moving the oracle one-hot from the state—same 18-dim width,
partitioned sampling unchanged—does prevent the partitioned
collapse (last-block mean |Q| 8.9 × 105 →467, loss peak
1.3 × 105 →20, catch recovering to 0.94), but both single-
buffer arms still diverge: the oracle signal is necessary for the
partitioned collapse yet not the sole cause of the single-buffer
one. The mechanism is real but partial.
F. F6: The Switch Point Lies Outside the Reachable Episode
Horizon
The protocol switches the opponent’s personality at step 100
of a 400-step episode, on the rationale that 20 HP makes the
switch observable before the fight ends; measuring episode
length contradicts this. Against the deployed opponent (a
competent chaser) mean episode length is 51.2 steps and the
longest observed is 78; against the random-walk opponent
used for classifier training, mean length is 323.2 and 20 of
24 episodes reach step 100.
No episode reaches the switch, so the mid-episode transition
the method is designed around does not occur. We bound the
effect rather than measure it: in our re-run at the original HP,
675 of 864 evaluation episodes ended before step 100, and at
the calibrated HP 60 it was 82 of 864. switch_step=40
raises switch-reachability to 862 of 864 evaluation episodes.
G. F7: The Deployed Classifier Is Calibrated to the Wrong
Opponent
The strategy classifier reaches 0.927 validation accuracy on
the random-walk opponent that generated its training data,
with per-class recall 0.942/0.832/0.979/0.953—an accuracy
that holds only in-distribution. Over 24 held-out episodes
per opponent, pre-switch label accuracy is 0.836 against the
random-walk opponent and falls to 0.306 against the com-
petent chaser the NPC is deployed against, with medians of
0.969 and 0.043—the latter below the 0.25 chance rate of a
four-way problem: label collapse, not mere degradation. Both
values are recomputed from the saved checkpoint and per-
episode records, so the gap is reproducible though the training
run’s own log does not survive.
Any result attributed to the detection pipeline—including
the original detector-labelled rows—inherits this error. We
close the loop by retraining the classifier on the compe-
tent chaser and re-running all 36 detector-labelled cells: no
cell moves significantly (exact signed-rank p ≥0.375)—
partitioned replay still fails to help. The retrain is only a
local repair: on held-out windows competent-chaser accuracy
rises 0.267 →0.802, but under the episode-level protocol it
recovers only 0.306 →0.441, and random-walk accuracy col-
lapses 0.836 →0.250 (chance)—the classifier specialises to
the deployment opponent. Report accuracy where the module
is deployed, not where it was trained.
VI. DISCUSSION AND REPORTING RECOMMENDATIONS
A. What the Audit Changes About the Claim
The claim does not survive: partitioned replay does not
measurably improve adaptation to a mid-encounter switch
here, at either difficulty, with the label withheld or supplied—
the effect is smaller than its baseline’s measurement noise.
What this does not show: one environment, one architecture,
six seeds, one switch per episode; partitioning might help
in regimes we did not test (more frequent switching, longer
horizons, weaker or more diverse opponents).
B. A Reporting Checklist
Each of the four infrastructure changes in Section IV was
necessary for at least one finding; we recommend seven items
for RL evaluations reporting a between-method difference.
Report the noise floor of your metric: evaluate one fixed
checkpoint repeatedly and publish the spread; if it approaches
the effect, the effect is not established (F1). Seed the baseline
as carefully as the treatment;a dedicated RNG stream per
subsystem avoids asymmetric noise between arms. Verify the
manipulated event occurs: publish the fraction of evaluation
episodes in which the switch, spawn, or trigger you study
actually happened (F6, much of F5). Check your task is not
saturated; a win rate at 1.000 cannot distinguish methods—
sweep difficulty and report the sweep. Validate perception
modules in deployment; report classifier accuracy against the
opponent the agent faces, not the one that generated its training
data (F7). Measure the variable you ablate; if an ablation
“controls for” capacity, report the capacity effect explicitly
rather than assuming it negligible (F4). Keep per-episode
records; aggregate tables conceal censoring, early termination,
and the difference between a median of zero and a mean of
0.056.
C. Reproducibility and Artifacts
Every number is checked against the raw records by a script;
plotted values are decoded from the figures and re-derived. We
release the audited system (environment, classifier, detector,

replay memory, agent), the pre-registered protocol, run-naming
scheme, record schema, per-figure audit table, and reporting
checklist—the audit can be repeated and the defects sought
elsewhere. The source compiles under IEEEtran within the
six-page limit.
D. Limitations
Our audit has the same weaknesses it criticizes: six seeds
per cell is few, so confidence intervals are wide and our
null result is a failure to detect an effect, not proof of its
absence. We localized the F5 collapse in the partitioned arm
to the oracle one-hot—removing it (same state width) restores
the partitioned arm to catch 0.94, while critic weight decay
does not—but the single-buffer divergence stays unexplained
and catch is already near zero before the divergence onset,
so cause and consequence cannot be separated. One origi-
nal configuration (ground-truth/cond-on) could not be re-run
(checkpoints overwritten), so F5 is characterized only where
artifacts survive; the Table I repeats and the 0.915 detector
log are gone—all reported as records rather than regenerable
evidence. Finally, the HP search was ours: the sweep is
reported in full and the selection rule was fixed before the
deciding run, but HP is itself a researcher degree of freedom
a future replication should vary.
VII. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
Strategy-conditioned partitioned experience replay is a rea-
sonable idea for NPCs facing a player who changes style mid-
encounter, but its reported +0.25 does not survive audit: it
is not recovered from the surviving checkpoints, is smaller
than its baseline’s own noise, and vanishes once the task
is calibrated away from saturation—the one comparison that
separated from zero is a training-collapse artifact that hit every
arm.
The durable contributions are the released testbed and the
failure analysis: an unreproducible baseline, a saturated metric,
an event that never occurs, and a classifier calibrated to the
wrong opponent are ordinary outcomes of small-sweep RL
work, each cheap to detect with the checklist (Section VI).
Future work: re-test the method where partitioning matters,
with the noise floor measured first.
